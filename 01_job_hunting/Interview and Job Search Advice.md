## General Advice
1. Interviewing isn’t about luck or memorizing scripted answers; it’s about skill.
2. The people who most often get hired aren’t the smartest. They’re the ones who know **how to think like hiring managers.** They treat interviews as a **transaction** where they’re selling a solution to one of the company’s biggest issues.
3. Study the company’s publicly available business problems instead of random trivia or fun facts. Figure out the following:
	1. Why does the role exist?
	2. How is the business losing time or money?
	3. How is your work meant to fix that?
		1. Read earnings reports and LinkedIn pages.
		2. Connect your past work to their financial goals.
4. Talk about what **delivered**, not what **did**.
	1. Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z.
	2. If you can’t quantify your work, they won’t hire you.
5. Structure every answer (Clarity is power)
	1. If you can’t explain something simply, you don’t understand it well enough. Structure your answers using methods such as STAR, CAR, or HERO.
	2. Make it effortless for the interviewer to follow your logic and evaluate your impact.
6. Look intentional (not pretentious)
	1. Look appropriate.
	2. Don’t have a messy background (virtual).
	3. Make sure your face is well lit.
	4. Have clean audio. Use **wired** headphones.
	5. Have your camera at eye level.
7. Show up early. Logistical readiness is mental readiness.
	1. For physical interviews, show up at least 10 minutes early.
	2. For virtual interviewers, join the call 5 minutes early.
	3. Check your microphone, camera, and connection.
8. Ask questions that reveal performance expectations.
	1. Don’t ask about company culture or other mundane facts. They don’t care and neither should you.
	2. What would make someone exceed expectations in this role within the first 6 months?
	3. What **measurable** outcomes define success here?
	4. Doing this helps you gather **meaningful**, **actionable** intelligence about the company.
9. Manage energy, not ego.
	1. Confidence is about demonstrating you understand your own value, not pretending you don’t need the job.
	2. Speak calmly and clearly about your results. This helps project authority.
10. End like a consultant.
	1. Don’t wait for approval. Finish the interview like a problem solver by showing that you’re already thinking like someone who’s in the role.
		1. It sounds like "problem" and "goal" are top of mind for your team. If I were starting next week, I’d focus on specific priority. Does that align with what you’re envisioning for this role?
	2. Asking this signals strategic thinking and partnership. You’re not asking for permission, you’re showing alignment.
11. Getting the offer isn’t about being perfect. It’s about managing perception. You want to signal that you’re low-risk, high-impact, and easy to trust. You’re not selling your past, you’re selling your future value.

## HERO Framework
- This framework helps answer interview questions in a concise and structured manner. The STAR (Situation Task Action Result) and CAR (Context Action Result) frameworks **are good for entry-level or mid-level roles**, but don’t make you sound like a leader ready to solve problems.
	- Use the STAR framework when applying for entry-level or mid-level Data Engineering roles.
	- Use the HERO framework when you're applying for or being promoted to senior roles or management (TPM) roles.
- Additionally, the STAR and CAR frameworks bury the impact by putting it at the end while the HERO framework puts it at the beginning.
- Think of the answer in terms of these three phases: Impact, then Judgement, then Execution.
---
1. **Headline:** Start with outcome. This should be done in one sentence that grabs the attention of the interviewer.
2. **Effect:** Mention why the outcome mattered. What business, user, or product problem did it solve?
3. **Rationale:**
	1. What options did you consider?
	2. Why did you make the decision you made?
	3. This showcases your judgment skills.
4. **Operations:** Talk about what you or your team actually did, including the method, tools, and execution.

## Mind Tricks to Make Interviews Easier
1. **You can’t subtract thoughts, only add:** You can’t ‘subtract’ negative thoughts by telling yourself not to think about those thoughts. Practice your answers  **out load**. Do mock interviews until your answers feel automatic. Managers will see this and think, **“if they prepared well for the interview, they’ll be good at the job.”**
2. **Anxiety is energy:** Anxiety and excitement trigger the same adrenaline response. Label your anxiety as ‘having a lot energy’. **Don’t go in too energized though**, remain grounded. **Use the bathroom before your interview.**
3. **Criticism is a chemical reaction:** Criticisms aren’t a definitive verdict of who you are. It’s just another person’s opinion of you. You know what they say about opinions…
	1. Don’t think about or hope to be judged well.
4. **Embarrassment can’t hurt you:** Anticipation of embarrassment is worse than the actual embarrassment. Handle it gracefully and recover. This actually looks good to the interviewer.
5. **Control the Controllable:** Only control what you can control. You can’t control the interviewer’s mood. You can only control:
	1. Your level of preparedness.
	2. The stories you have.
	3. How you answer them.
	4. How you walk into the room, sit down, and compose yourself.
	5. **Don’t perform. Communicate.**
6. **Interviewing is a skill, not a personality trait:** Interviewing will feel awkward and uncomfortable at first. It’s like learning how to ride a bike.

## Hiring Manager Insights
1. Sometimes you’re a benchmark, not a contender. This happens when a hiring manager is considering a referral or an internal promotion, but policy requires them to run a fair process.
	1. If you can make a strong impression in this scenario, you become a front runner if things go a different way.
2. The silver medalist usually gets the stronger offer.
	1. If you don’t get the offer, it doesn’t necessarily mean someone else did.
	2. Handle rejection professionally, show maturity, and keep the door open.
3. Desperation makes them cautious, not generous.
	1. When a hiring manager is desperate to fill a role, it makes them cautious about **hiring the wrong person**, not generous in terms of hiring an under-qualified candidate.
4. Most of the interview is confirmation bias.
	1. Managers usually decide within the first 5 minutes if your a yes or a no. The rest of the time, they’re trying to confirm that decision.
	2. Project confidence and clarity within the first 5 minutes of your interview.
	3. Show up steady, clear, and grounded from the start. This matters more than scripting perfect answers.
	4. In an interview, try to act like you already work there (like it’s a performance review). Be relaxed, focused, curious, not eager or robotic.
5. Being overqualified is more about risk than ego.
	1. When a manager tells you that you were overqualified, they’re afraid you won’t stick around or you made someone on the team feel insecure.

## Resume Advice
1. Preparation:
	1. Study the current job market. **Without doing proper research, you’ll end up creating a generic resume.**
	2. Research the companies and job titles you want to apply for. Look for patterns and craft your resume to fit those patterns.
	3. Market your past experience and achievements.
	4. Professional Summary:
		1. Begin with a clear statement of your professional capabilities.
		2. Follow this with a value proposition that includes evidence.
		3. Add a memorable trait that makes you stand out, like an award or unique project.
		4. Clearly state why you’re applying for the position.
		5. Spell check and **save as a PDF.**
	5. **Things to Avoid:**
		1. Images, Graphics, Clip Art, Columns, and Tables.
		2. Don’t stuff your resume with keywords just to get past the ATS. Use keywords strategically throughout your resume. **Assume that a human will actually read it at some point, not just a computer.**
2. Managers want someone who is **safe**, not necessarily the person with the best resume.
	1. They want someone who can step in and solve problems without creating more. **They simply want someone who will make their job easier.**
3. Every bullet on your resume should make you look like someone who’s already doing the job. The bullets about your experience should be detailed (include dates and metrics), not vague.
4. Explain risk (such as long gaps in work history or short tenure within a particular role) in your resume summary.
5. Translate past experiences into business priorities.
6. In your bullet points, lead with verbs that show ownership.
7. Only highlight the last 5 to 7 years of experience on your resume, with more emphasis on more recent experience.
8. Balance Technical Credibility, Business Impact, and Leadership so your resume is well-rounded.

## Tell Me About Yourself
1. When the interviewer asks this question, they want to know why you will be a good fit for the job.
2. Answering this question involves the following prep work:
	1. Look at the job description and read all of the responsibilities.
	2. Think of the job description as a list of problems the business needs to solve. You need to pitch yourself as the solution.
	3. When you don’t have direct experience, talk about how you would solve a problem hypothetically. Explain how you have the right skills and can learn quickly.
	4. The first part of the answer is the “log line”:
		1. In the first sentence, talk about who you are as a professional
		2. In the second sentence, talk about how you would be a good fit for the job.
		3. Keeping the introduction short will leave a good first impression.
3. Next take 2-3 sentences to talk about what you’ve done in your prior roles. Tie this experience to the job you’re applying for.
4. In the last sentence, explain why the job is good next step for you.
5. End with this crucial closer.
	1. I am excited to share more about myself during this interview. Given our limited time together, can you tell me what you’d like to learn most about me? That way I can give you information that is most relevant to you and this position.

## Lies You Can Tell During an Interview
1. How are you?
	1. I’m great, thank you. How are you?
2. Why do you want to work here?
	1. Tell them what you’d like to provide for them instead of what they can provide for you.
	2. Say something about how you’ve outgrown your current/previous role and are looking for a new challenge. Tell them about the team’s goals you’ve researched and how you can potentially help solve them.
3. Where do you see yourself in 5 years?
	1. When hiring managers ask this what they’re really asking is:
		1. Are you gonna leave in 6 months?
		2. Are your goals aligned with this role, or are you going to get bored and become a problem?
	2. Tell them you see yourself at that company, becoming an expert in this area able to quickly solve problems and innovate. Tell them you see yourself as a valuable part of the team. This role aligns with where I want to go because… **[something specific about the role/job.]**
		1. With this, you’re not lying. You’re telling a version of the truth that’s relevant to the conversation.
4. Why are you looking for a new opportunity (if you’re employed)?
	1. Don’t mention that you hate your job or the company. This will only serve to make the hiring manager more cautious about you, thinking about how you’ll feel about their company in the future.
	2. Instead, say that you learned a lot in your current role and are ready for a new challenge.
5. How do you feel about your current manager?
	1. **As with the previous question, don’t mention anything negative. The hiring manager might become your manager.**
	2. Instead mention the positive aspects about your manager and team, what you’ve learned, and how you’re excited to bring that experience to your new role.
6. What are your hobbies?
	1. Paint an interesting picture of who you are while you’re not at work. Pick the version of yourself that’s true and memorable.
	2. Mention how you like to cook and how that potentially relates to the role.
7. Tell me about your previous job title and description?
	1. Don’t undersell what you’ve done. If you’ve been doing work above your pay grade, mention it.
8. Resume Gaps
	1. Don’t stress about. They saw the gap and called you in for an interview anyways.
	2. The gap isn’t the problem, how you explain it can be potentially be a problem.
	3. **Tell them you got laid off due to cost cutting.**
9. Do you have any questions for us?
	1. **Don’t say no and that you think they covered everything.** This communicates that you’re not very interested in the role, that you haven’t thought beyond today, and that you’re just trying to get through the interview.
	2. Have **at least** two questions ready:
		1. Why is this position open?
		2. What does success look like in the first 90 days?
		3. You mentioned the team is growing. Can you elaborate on the relationship between this role and the team it collaborates with the most?
		4. What is the most significant challenge someone in this role would need to tackle in their first month?
	3. **Don’t ask about anything benefits-related.** That’s a negotiation discussion, not an interview discussion.

## How to Get a Job Post-Layoff
1. A layoff is an emotional event while a job search is strategic.
2. Financial Triage:
	1. Job searches take an average of 3 to 9 months, even for top performers
	2. Get written proof about why you were terminated.
	3. File for unemployment immediately.
	4. Review and negotiate severance.
3. Define Your Narrative:
	1. Write down all of the wins you accomplished while working there.
	2. Select 3-4 quantifiable stories about your performance.
	3. Craft a clean, neutral layoff line.
		1. Example: “My role was eliminated due to company-wide restructuring. I’m now looking for new opportunities where I can apply my experience with what I’ve learned.”
4. Re-Activate Your Network:
	1. Warm networks are a better asset than applying online. Trust accelerates hiring.
	2. Start with former mentors or managers.
	3. Use low-pressure messages to ask for help. Don’t necessarily ask for a job. Ask for advice.
5. Stick to a Routine
	1. Make a daily plan for your job search and stick to it.
	2. For example:
		1. Find 2-3 target companies you want to work for and look up their mission and jobs pages.
		2. Send out 1-2 personalized outreach messages.
		3. Make a small update to your LinkedIn and/or resume.
		4. Practice at least 1 interview question and answer.

## Getting Hired Without Experience
1. The job market is broken. ‘Entry-level’ doesn’t mean the company is hiring complete beginners with no professional experience.
2. Entry-level roles aren’t built for beginners, they’re built for ‘plug and play’ hires. Companies used to train employees internally. Learning and development budgets were re-directed toward external recruitment because it is a lot cheaper.
3. Companies outsource talent, they don’t grow talent.
4. Catch-22 of Experience: Self-made experience doesn’t count.
	1. True ‘entry-level’ roles are instead labeled as internships or apprenticeships.
	2. Trainee (Associate or Representative) programs don’t require prior experience, but do require learning agility.
	3. ‘Returnships’: Meant for people re-entering the job market after a break. Often targeted towards caregivers, former entrepreneurs, or anyone with a resume gap.
5. Companies rarely consider freelance or gig work as professional experience.
	1. Highlight freelance work as measurable impact, not just ‘deliverables’.
	2. Use testimonials and LinkedIn endorsements to add credibility.
	3. Have a visual portfolio of your work ready.
	4. **Show what you did, who you helped, and the outcomes of your work. Don’t just mention ‘you’re a freelancer’.**
6. When looking for true entry-level work, apply for:
	1. Associate or Junior titles.
	2. Trainee or Rotational Programs.
	3. Support Representative.
	4. Client Success.
	5. Service Coordinator.
	6. Startups and Early-Stage Companies.
	7. **Apply to be trainable, not to be ready to do the work.**
7. Post experience, not matter how small, on LinkedIn. Even small Coursera credentials count towards credibility.
